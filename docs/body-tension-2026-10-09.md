For today I’d make the whole session about one idea:

**Body tension = connecting hands and feet through the trunk so force can travel through the whole body.**

For intermediate climbers, I’d avoid too much anatomy and teach them to feel 3 useful “chains”:

- **Front chain:** abs, hip flexors, quads. Useful when keeping feet on steep terrain and pulling knees toward the wall.
- **Back chain:** calves, hamstrings, glutes, spinal extensors. Very relevant for heel hooks and driving the hips in.
- **Diagonal / rotational chain:** shoulder, obliques, opposite hip and leg. Important for flagging, cross moves, toe hooks and resisting rotation.

## 1. Warm-up: feel the chains, 15 min

### A. Wall tension test
On very easy overhang:
1. Establish four points.
2. Relax the body deliberately.
3. Then tense feet, legs, glutes and trunk progressively.
4. Release one hand.
5. Repeat with different feet.

Ask:
**“Where did you have to create tension to stop rotating?”**

This is a good introduction because they immediately feel the difference between “holding with arms” and “connecting the body.”

### B. Opposite hand-foot
Climb easy terrain while focusing on:
- right hand + left foot
- left hand + right foot

Pause before each move and feel the diagonal connection.

Then deliberately try same-side hand/foot positions and compare.

### C. Hover drill
Before moving a hand:
- release it
- hover for 2 seconds
- then make the move

If they cannot hover, their feet/body are not producing enough stability.

# 2. Body tension exercises

## Exercise 1: No-cut climbing
Use an easy/moderate overhang.

Rule:
**Both feet may never simultaneously leave the wall.**

Progression:
1. Large feet.
2. Smaller feet.
3. Longer hand moves.
4. Remove one foothold.

Coaching cues:
- push through toes
- pull hips toward wall
- tense before moving
- transfer tension before releasing the hand

Watch for people who tense only their abs. Explain that tension should often start at the feet.

---

## Exercise 2: Three-point tension

Choose a steep wall with good holds.

From a stable position:
- remove one hand
- hold 3 seconds
- replace it
- remove one foot
- hold 3 seconds

Ask them where tension changes.

Good question:
**“Which remaining limb suddenly has to work harder?”**

This teaches force distribution.

---

## Exercise 3: Cut loose vs keep feet

Use the same move twice.

Attempt A:
Allow feet to cut.

Attempt B:
Keep feet connected.

Then compare:
- hand force
- swing
- recovery effort
- grip effort

This makes body tension very obvious.

---

# 3. Heel hook block

The important lesson:

**A heel hook is not just placing the heel. It is pulling through the heel and connecting it to the hips.**

## Exercise 4: Passive heel vs active heel

Put them in an easy position with a clear heel hook.

First:
Place heel but do not actively pull.

Then:
- dig heel down
- flex knee
- engage hamstring
- squeeze glute
- pull hip toward heel

Release one hand.

They should immediately feel the difference.

Ask:
**“Can you make your hand almost unnecessary?”**

---

## Exercise 5: Heel hook hand unloading

On easy terrain:
1. Establish heel.
2. Gradually reduce grip pressure.
3. Open one hand.
4. Eventually release it.

Progressions:
- lower heel
- higher heel
- heel farther to the side

This helps them understand how heel position changes force direction.

---

## Exercise 6: Heel angle experiment

Use one hold.

Try heel:
- close to body
- far away
- high
- low

Do not change hands.

Ask:
**“Which position pulls your hips toward the wall, and which one pulls you off?”**

Very useful for understanding mechanics rather than memorising beta.

---

## Exercise 7: Heel hook + rock-over

Sequence:
1. Place heel.
2. Pull body toward heel.
3. Raise hips.
4. Transition from pulling with heel to weighting the foot.
5. Stand up.

This is particularly useful because real climbing often uses a heel hook as a transition, not a final position.

---

# 4. Toe hook block

Key concept:

**Toe hooks resist the body moving away from the wall or rotating.**

The chain is usually something like:

**toe → shin → hip → obliques → opposite shoulder**

## Exercise 8: Toe hook stabilisation

Easy roof.

Establish:
- two hands
- one normal foot
- one toe hook

Without changing anything:
1. Relax toe hook.
2. Feel body rotate.
3. Activate toe hook.
4. Feel rotation stop.

Repeat several times.

This is probably the clearest toe-hook drill.

---

## Exercise 9: Toe hook hand release

Establish toe hook and progressively:
- loosen one hand
- open fingers
- release hand completely

If they cannot release:
change toe position or hip position.

Teach:
**Toe hook + body position must work together.**

---

## Exercise 10: Toe hook resistance

Partner lightly pulls the climber's hips away from the wall while they maintain an easy toe-hook position.

Very gentle, not a strength contest.

The climber should feel:
- toe dorsiflexion
- shin
- hip flexor
- abs/obliques

This makes the muscle chain very tangible.

---

# 5. Best exercise: heel vs toe experiment

Find one feature that allows both.

Have them try:

1. Heel hook.
2. Toe hook.
3. Normal foot.
4. No foot.

Same hand position each time.

Ask:

**What movement is each foot preventing?**

That question gets to the core difference.

Heel typically:
**pulls your body toward the hold / supports hip elevation.**

Toe typically:
**prevents rotation or your body swinging away.**

---

# 6. Heel-to-toe transition

Very useful intermediate exercise.

Start with heel hook.

Then:
1. Pull hips in.
2. Stabilise.
3. Rotate foot.
4. Convert heel into toe hook.
5. Move body underneath/away.

Then reverse it.

It teaches that hooks are not isolated techniques. They are ways of manipulating force direction.

---

# 7. Finish with constrained bouldering

For the final 30 minutes, choose 3–4 appropriate boulders.

Give one constraint per attempt:

**Attempt 1:** no cutting feet.  
**Attempt 2:** exaggerate body tension.  
**Attempt 3:** identify one diagonal muscle chain.  
**Attempt 4:** find a heel hook, even if it isn't optimal.  
**Attempt 5:** find a toe hook or explain why one would not work.  
**Attempt 6:** climb normally using what they learned.

This last step is important. They should learn **when not to use** a heel or toe hook too.

### The three coaching cues I'd repeat all session

**“What is pulling, and what is pushing?”**

**“What movement is this foot preventing?”**

**“Can you reduce pressure on your hands?”**

Those three questions will teach body tension better than giving them twenty different techniques.
